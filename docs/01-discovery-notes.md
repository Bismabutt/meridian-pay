# Discovery Notes

Findings from the discovery phase, before any architecture was drawn. Each
finding is recorded with the design decision it drove, because the platform
is a response to these problems rather than a greenfield exercise.

## Context

Meridian Pay is a UK electronic money institution. It holds no banking
licence. Customer funds sit in a single pooled safeguarding account at a
partner bank, and the ledger is the only record of how that pool divides
between customers. If the ledger is wrong, the business cannot prove whose
money is whose, which is a regulatory failure rather than a defect.

**Scale:** 1.2 million business accounts, roughly 40 million ledger entries
growing by 2 million a month.

**Traffic:** 40 payments per second at baseline, 400 to 500 on payday
mornings. Balance reads run at roughly 3,000 per second, outnumbering
payments six to one.

**Data residency:** United Kingdom.

---

## Traffic and scale

**1. Payday morning produces a twelvefold rise within fifteen minutes.**
The first working morning after month end, every business runs payroll and
supplier payments at once. Capacity that is adequate at 09:00 is inadequate
by 09:15.

*Design implication:* horizontal autoscaling with a scale up target low
enough that capacity arrives before customers feel the spike. See the HPA
configuration in the Helm values.

**2. Read traffic exceeds payment traffic six to one.**
Customers check balances far more often than they move money.

*Design implication:* account-service carries the highest replica count and
the most aggressive autoscaling bounds, despite being Tier 2.

**3. Load is geographically concentrated.**
All customers are UK businesses operating in one timezone, so there is no
natural traffic smoothing across regions.

*Design implication:* single region deployment in eu-west-2, with capacity
sized for a sharp national peak rather than a rolling global one.

---

## Failure history

**4. A bad release took three hours to recover.**
There was no rollback procedure. The engineer who knew the recovery steps
was on leave. For three hours, no customer could move money.

*Design implication:* GitOps deployment with a single command rollback, and
a recorded drill proving the recovery time.

**5. Deployments were manual.**
Someone connected over SSH and copied files onto servers.

*Design implication:* no human deploys anything. The pipeline builds, the
cluster reconciles to what is in the repository.

**6. An expired TLS certificate was discovered through customer complaints
on social media.**
Nobody was watching certificate expiry.

*Design implication:* certificate management automated, and an observability
floor that surfaces problems before customers do.

**7. An analytics query was run directly against the production ledger
database and degraded payment latency for approximately an hour.**
Everything in the network could reach everything else.

*Design implication:* default deny NetworkPolicy into the money zone. Not a
guideline, an enforced boundary.

**8. The partner bank had two thirty minute outages in six months.**
During those windows payments were rejected outright. Customers who saw a
failure submitted again, which is how duplicate payments occur.

*Design implication:* accept, queue and retry rather than reject. A queued
payment is a success from the customer's point of view.

---

## Team and process

**9. Tests ran when someone remembered to run them.**
There was no automated gate.

*Design implication:* tests and security scanning run on every pull request,
and a failing gate blocks the merge rather than producing a warning.

**10. There was no staging environment.**
Changes were validated in production.

*Design implication:* the entire environment is Terraform, so an identical
environment can be created and destroyed on demand.

**11. Database credentials were held in a shared spreadsheet.**
Former employees retained access.

*Design implication:* credentials generated by Terraform, stored in AWS
Secrets Manager, never typed or seen by a human, different per database.

**12. Credentials were also committed to the repository.**
An `.env` file containing AWS keys was in version control.

*Design implication:* secret scanning across full git history in the
pipeline, not only the current working tree.

**13. Recovery procedures lived in individual engineers' heads.**
Local development setup required asking a specific colleague.

*Design implication:* one command brings the whole platform up locally. The
knowledge is in the repository, not in a person.

---

## Compliance and dependencies

**14. An auditor requested deployment history and the team reconstructed it
from chat messages.**
There was no authoritative record of who deployed what and when.

*Design implication:* every production change is a reviewed commit. Control
plane audit logging enabled. The git history is the audit trail.

**15. Production data must never be copied into non production
environments.**
GDPR, and the data includes financial records for real businesses.

*Design implication:* synthetic data generated by seeder scripts at
realistic scale. No production extract ever leaves production.

**16. The identity provider used for KYC checks is a third party with its
own availability profile.**
A failed KYC check blocks onboarding but must not block existing customers.

*Design implication:* KYC failures delay onboarding rather than failing it.
The dependency is isolated from the payment path.

**17. The FX rates provider is a third party whose rates are cached.**
A provider outage must not stop currency conversion entirely.

*Design implication:* serve stale rates with a visible age rather than
failing. Staleness is a business decision, not a technical one.
